This perspective article from Diabetes Care, titled Adult-Onset Type 1 Diabetes: Current Understanding and Challenges, summarizes the outcomes of an international JDRF expert workshop aimed at improving the clinical understanding, diagnostic accuracy, and management of adult-onset type 1 diabetes. The primary objective of the workshop and paper is to address the widespread issue of misdiagnosis, as over half of all new type 1 diabetes cases occur in adults, yet nearly 40% are initially misclassified—most commonly as type 2 diabetes due to slower metabolic progression and overlapping clinical traits. By highlighting the distinct epidemiological, immunogenetic, and metabolic features of adult-onset cases, the authors seek to establish clearer diagnostic frameworks and treatment guidelines for practicing clinicians. To evaluate potential cases effectively, the authors provide the diagnostic "AABBCC" framework, guiding clinicians to systematically assess Age at onset, Autoimmunity via islet autoantibody testing, Body habitus and BMI, family and personal background, glycemic Control trajectory, and associated Comorbidities such as thyroid autoimmunity or celiac disease. For laboratory guidelines, the authors recommend ordering islet autoantibody assays as the primary test for short-duration disease (under three years), with serum glutamic acid decarboxylase autoantibodies serving as the predominant marker. For disease durations exceeding three years, C-peptide testing paired with blood glucose is recommended as the main tool to measure remaining beta-cell function. Regarding therapeutic management, the paper outlines guidelines based on C-peptide levels and disease characteristics. Patients with random C-peptide levels at or below 300 pmol/L require primary insulin therapy. For individuals with higher C-peptide levels or non-insulin-requiring disease, clinicians are instructed to exercise caution with non-insulin agents, explicitly advising that sulfonylureas be avoided because they can accelerate beta-cell failure. Additionally, the guidelines warn against SGLT1 and SGLT2 inhibitors due to a heightened risk of diabetic ketoacidosis in adult-onset type 1 diabetes, particularly in non-obese individuals taking low insulin doses.
