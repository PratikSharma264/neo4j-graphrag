The American Diabetes Association (ADA) position statement on the evaluation and management of youth-onset type 2 diabetes provides comprehensive guidelines on the pathophysiology, screening, diagnostic approaches, and treatment goals for this condition. Youth-onset type 2 diabetes arises when pancreatic $\beta$-cells fail to secrete adequate insulin to compensate for severe insulin resistance. This disease trajectory is heavily influenced by obesity and the transient, physiological insulin resistance that naturally occurs during puberty. Notably, young individuals display significantly higher insulin resistance for a given degree of adiposity and experience a much faster decline in $\beta$-cell function compared to adults.To identify affected individuals early, risk-based screening is recommended after age 10 or the onset of puberty for children who are overweight (BMI $\ge$ 85th percentile) or obese (BMI $\ge$ 95th percentile) and possess at least one additional risk factor. These risk factors include maternal history of diabetes or gestational diabetes, a family history of type 2 diabetes in 1st- or 2nd-degree relatives, high-risk racial/ethnic background, or clinical signs of insulin resistance such as acanthosis nigricans, hypertension, dyslipidemia, or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS). Screening can be performed using fasting plasma glucose, a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), or A1C, and should be repeated at least every 3 years—or more frequently if BMI increases. Because obesity has also become prevalent in children with type 1 diabetes, testing for pancreatic autoantibodies (such as GAD65 and IA2) is critical to exclude autoimmune type 1 diabetes, while genetic evaluation should be considered if monogenic diabetes is suspected.Management of youth-onset type 2 diabetes centers around individualized glycemic targets, patient-family education, and multidisciplinary care. A1C should be monitored every 3 months, with a standard goal of < 7.0% for those on oral medications alone, or a tighter target of < 6.5% in select cases (such as short disease duration or significant weight improvement) where it can be achieved without significant hypoglycemia. Comprehensive, culturally competent diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES) involving both the patient and their family is foundational to treatment. Finally, for severely obese adolescents whose blood glucose remains uncontrolled despite lifestyle and medical therapy, metabolic/bariatric surgery can be considered when provided by an experienced multidisciplinary team.
