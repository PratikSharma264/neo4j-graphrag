The study Glycemic control and complications in patients with type 1 diabetes – a registry-based longitudinal study of adolescents and young adults evaluates longitudinal glycemic control, the prevalence of microvascular complications (retinopathy and nephropathy), and the impact of transition timing from pediatric to adult care among 874 young people with type 1 diabetes in Norway using data linked from two nationwide registries. The study found that median HbA1c peaked in late adolescence at 9.3% for females at age 17 and 9.1% for males at age 19, with overall target HbA1c levels achieved by very few participants (only 11% of adolescents aged 14–17 and 7% of young adults aged 18–25). Microvascular complications were prevalent, with 16% of participants exhibiting retinopathy and 13% exhibiting nephropathy; higher HbA1c levels across adolescence and young adulthood, longer diabetes duration, younger age at diagnosis, and smoking were strongly associated with a higher risk of complications. Additionally, patients who transferred to adult care before age 18 had significantly higher HbA1c levels and a higher prevalence of complications than those who transferred between ages 18–22, while less than 40% of patients with albuminuria were receiving recommended ACE inhibitor or ARB treatment. Based on these findings, the paper recommends that pediatricians and endocrinologists critically assess the care offered to adolescents and young adults, adopt new approaches to help improve glycemic control during the vulnerable late-teen years, optimize transition strategies to adult care, and strictly adhere to clinical guidelines regarding blood pressure and kidney protection medications for patients with nephropathy.
