In the paper When Does A Minor's Legal Competence To Make Health Care Decisions Matter?, Lois A. Weithorn evaluates the role of minors' legal competence in American healthcare decision-making, examining the default doctrine of parental consent alongside statutory and judicial exceptions such as the mature minor doctrine. The analysis finds that a minor's decisional competence is rarely the primary trigger for transferring legal authority; instead, courts and legislatures first assess whether policy goals or constitutional rights justify bypassing parental authority before assessing whether capable minors should decide for themselves. Furthermore, psychological and neuroscientific research indicates that cognitive capacities necessary for logical "cold cognition" reasoning reach adult levels by early to mid-adolescence (around ages 12 to 15), while psychosocial maturity in emotional "hot cognition" contexts continues to develop into adulthood. Based on these findings, the author recommends recognizing that legal authority rules reflect deliberate policy balances rather than simple reflections of developmental maturity, and guidelines emphasize promoting minors' participation as collaborative partners in medical decisions alongside parents and healthcare providers, regardless of strict legal boundaries
