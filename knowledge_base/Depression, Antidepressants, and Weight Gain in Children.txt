"Depression, Antidepressants, and Weight Gain in Children" evaluates the independent and interactive effects of depression diagnosis, antidepressant treatment, and various health comorbidities on body mass index (BMI) trajectories over time using electronic health record data from a large sample of children. The commentary highlights findings from Schwartz et al., showing that both a prior depression diagnosis and antidepressant therapy are significantly and independently associated with increases in BMI, with one year of antidepressant exposure resulting in an average weight gain of 2.1 kg (4.6 lbs) overall and 2.5 kg specifically for SSRIs. While this weight gain is lower than the 11.6 kg mean increase associated with antipsychotic medications in children, it remains clinically significant and may elevate the risk of metabolic disease. Based on these observations and the potential for persistent disease effects, the paper recommends that children diagnosed with depression—whether receiving antidepressant therapy or not—should be carefully monitored for weight gain, alongside exploring risk-mitigation strategies to prevent associated metabolic issues.
