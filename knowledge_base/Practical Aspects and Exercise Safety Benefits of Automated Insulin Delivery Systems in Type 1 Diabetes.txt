Practical Aspects and Exercise Safety Benefits of Automated Insulin Delivery Systems in Type 1 Diabetes evaluates the effectiveness, operational mechanisms, and practical management strategies of automated insulin delivery (AID) systems during and after exercise in individuals with type 1 diabetes. The authors find that while AID technology modestly improves time in range and reduces hypoglycemia during physical activity, inherent limitations in the pharmacodynamics of subcutaneously administered rapid-acting insulins prevent systems from fully adjusting to sudden exercise-induced glycemic shifts or rapid changes in insulin requirements. To optimize safety and glycemic control, the paper recommends setting elevated glucose targets (typically 140–160 mg/dL or system-specific exercise/activity modes) starting 1–2 hours prior to planned exercise lasting 30 minutes or longer, and extending these targets into post-exercise recovery or overnight when necessary to prevent delayed hypoglycemia. Additionally, guidelines suggest reducing mealtime bolus doses by 25–75% for exercise occurring within 1–3 hours after meals, using perceived exertion scales (like the modified Borg scale) to differentiate aerobic activities requiring higher targets from anaerobic exercises that may trigger temporary glycemic spikes, and utilizing carbohydrate supplementation or temporary pump suspensions during unplanned or spontaneous physical activity.
